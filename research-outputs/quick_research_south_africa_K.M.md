## The finding that matters most

Two injectable cancer medicines have **confirmed South African supply disruptions** in the review period:
**carboplatin 450 mg per 45 ml**, where manufacturing delays forced sourcing from an alternative supplier, and
**docetaxel 20 mg per ml**, where manufacturing constraints led to paclitaxel being used as a clinical alternative.
The source is the Health Minister's reply to a parliamentary question, reported in July 2026, in which he also said
that oncology medicine supply "remains challenging due to reliance on a limited number of international
manufacturers". Oncology was named alongside antibiotics, tuberculosis medicines, insulin and some paediatric
antiretrovirals as categories frequently affected.

**No South African shortage was found** for methotrexate, ifosfamide, azacitidine, oxaliplatin, irinotecan,
gemcitabine, bendamustine, pemetrexed, decitabine or arsenic trioxide.

## There is no South African shortage register

South Africa has no public medicine shortage database comparable to the United States or European ones. Public
sector availability is tracked internally through RxSolution and the Stock Visibility System, which report weekly to
the National Surveillance Centre. The National Department of Health publishes supply alerts and takes reports at a
stock alert mailbox. SAHPRA's Rapid Alert System carries safety alerts, recalls and quality notices, not shortages,
and SAHPRA's main shortage instrument is the Section 21 authorisation that permits importing an unregistered
medicine when the registered one is unavailable.

A BMJ Global Health study found that access to 7 of 25 priority childhood cancer medicines in South Africa was
potentially constrained because procurement was unsuccessful.

**No published South African analysis maps injectable cancer medicine supply chains to specific overseas
manufacturing risks.** The research looked for one at SAHPRA, the National Department of Health, Statistics South
Africa and the Health Systems Trust and did not find one.

## Who controls the patients

Oncology dispensing volume is gated by Designated Service Provider contracts, not by patient choice of pharmacy.

- **ICON Oncology** is the largest oncology provider: 161 oncologists, 66 chemotherapy facilities, 27 radiation
  facilities, and contracts with 32 or more medical schemes. It is vertically integrated and runs **its own
  formulary and treatment protocols**, so it controls where patients are treated, which medicines are used, and at
  what cost. Its Designated Service Provider status with Transmed continues into 2026.
- **Discovery Health** keeps its own oncology provider network lists; on some plans full cover depends on using a
  network oncologist. From January 2025 it introduced **reference pricing for cancer medicines that have generic
  alternatives**, requiring the best-priced generic to be dispensed, and it confirmed that oncology pharmacy
  networks remain in place for 2025.

A courier pharmacy therefore wins oncology work by being inside a scheme network or by contracting with a managed
oncology organisation, and loses it when a scheme designates a different network and patients face co-payments.

## What the price rules allow

- The **Single Exit Price** is the single maximum price at which a manufacturer may sell a medicine to any buyer,
  made up of an ex-manufacturer price, a logistics fee and Value Added Tax. **Discounts and rebates below the
  gazetted price are prohibited.**
- The annual adjustment is set by the Minister of Health on a formula weighted 70 per cent to local consumer price
  inflation and 30 per cent to exchange rate movement.
- **2026: an annual adjustment of 1.47 per cent**, gazetted 28 November 2025, **plus an extraordinary 2.88 per cent**
  from 1 October 2026. The exact 2025 percentage could not be extracted from the gazettes found.
- **Dispensing fee**, as updated on 27 August 2026 and excluding Value Added Tax, in four bands: up to R159.01,
  R23.13 plus 46 per cent; R159.02 to R423.55, R42.91 plus 33 per cent; R423.56 to R1,530.72, R122.59 plus 15 per
  cent; **R1,530.73 and above, R270.54 plus 5 per cent**. Most injectable cancer medicines fall in the top band, so
  the rand fee grows with price but the percentage margin is thin.
- **No stock-holding rules were found.** No regulation on buying ahead or holding buffer stock of price-regulated
  medicines was identified. The ban on buying below the Single Exit Price means **there is no financial gain from
  holding deeper stock** — the case for it has to rest on continuity of treatment, not on margin.

## What schemes pay for cancer

- Discovery Health Medical Scheme paid about **R83 billion** in total claims in 2024. The **average annual cost per
  cancer claimant reached R143,000 in 2024**, up from R114,000 in 2019, a 25 per cent rise in five years, and 94 per
  cent of members received full cover for cancer treatment.
- On the most recent detailed breakdown, from 2017, 38,295 members were in active cancer treatment, a rate of
  **1,394 per 100,000 lives and a 45 per cent rise in prevalence since 2011**, with 7,597 newly diagnosed.
- Screening by 2024 against 2019: prostate up 8.9 per cent, colorectal up 26.3 per cent, cervical still 9.1 per cent
  below.
- 2026 cancer cover thresholds run from R250,000 to R500,000 depending on plan, after which approved non-prescribed
  treatment continues at 80 per cent of the scheme rate.
- The Government Employees Medical Scheme lists an oncology limit of R425,863 per family per year on some options,
  with R312,895 of that for biological and specialised medicines.

## How exposed the country is to overseas manufacturing

- About **65 per cent of medicines consumed in South Africa are imported** (Black Pharmaceutical Industry
  Association); imports are more than two-thirds of pharmaceutical sales, and the top five source countries are
  about 60 per cent of imports (InvestSA).
- About **98 per cent of the active ingredients** used in local manufacturing are imported, costing roughly R15
  billion a year.
- Source countries, **on 2017 trade data**, are India 26 per cent, France 12 per cent, Germany 11 per cent, the
  United States 5 per cent and Ireland 4 per cent. **This split is nine years old and should be treated as
  indicative only.**
- The National Department of Health's joint statement with the industry in August 2026 acknowledged "heightened
  global geopolitical uncertainty and global supply-chain disruptions".

## What this does not show

The two South African disruptions are **not** shown to be caused by, or predicted by, the overseas shortage data
held in the datasets. They arrive from a separate source, they concern specific presentations, and nothing in this
research connects them to the United States, Italian, French or European listings. The absence of reported South
African shortages for the other ten medicines is **not** evidence that their supply is secure, because the country
publishes no shortage register in which such a shortage would appear.
